# Critical Reflection — Bailey Cooper
## COMM2651 Digital Writing 2026

Throughout the whole of this project, I have been attempting to negotiate between conceptual ambition and technical capacity. My initial vision for the text city was considerably more expansive — one that was to be a fully interactive and navigable space, where an AI model trained on my own writing would generate language in real time, so as to erect the cityscape anew on each visit. The end product was instead a simplified but functional version of that vision. Having the AI model interact with the city each time was ultimately abandoned for the final project, as it would have required me to host it. The compromise was eight pre-generated pieces which could then be selected by the viewer. Each constraint helped to bring clarity to the project, even if I was disappointed by the results. The version of the project that ran locally had a quality that the final one does not — watching the city be built in real time was immensely satisfying, and that remains something I intend to develop further.

The project is considerably self-archiving and ontological in the manner it was conceived. The AI model was trained on around 90,000 words of my own writing. This correlates heavily with Derrida's notion of archive fever — the desire to return to the origin, gathering and preserving what might otherwise dissipate. The model, crude as it at times was — at one disturbing point generating nothing but the word 'FACELESS' in capitals, repeatedly — did manage to capture, to a degree, my themes and the accompanying style. Available on the personal website created for this course under the heading 'Personal Writing' are three pieces of my writing that were used to train the model. If one were to compare those with the output of the model, I'm sure they too would see the similarity present.

When considering this transference of the more personal and ontological into something more representative of today's world, I am comfortable saying that the work sits naturally within the generative and algorithmic tradition of electronic literature. Flores' account of second and third generation E-Lit, where code becomes a co-author and the reader's interaction is a reconfiguring of the text, describes the logic of my piece well. The viewer's ability to select from eight texts and rebuild the cityscape is not dissimilar from the combinatory logic of works like Taroko Gorge or the Dada Remix we completed in class.

As for my more personal inspirations, as documented previously, Borges' Library of Babel remains the more fully realised influence than Gladman's work. My piece manages to carry some of the same overwhelming and disorienting accumulation of language that characterises Borges' work — the sense that meaning is present but inexhaustible due to the nature of its presentation. The inspiration of Gladman's Ravicka series, with its more fluid and atmospheric prose-architecture, is less noticeable in the final piece. Her work has an organic quality in the sense that language and place are inseparable, whilst in my work they still retain a distinct nature, the language component having been made to fit the space given it.
